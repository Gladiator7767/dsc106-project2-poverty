What counts as progress against poverty?

Proposition

Between 1990 and 2018, Sub-Saharan Africa made substantial progress against extreme poverty.

Looking at different metrics, you get different impressions. A lower proportion of people lived below the poverty line in 2018 than in 1990. The average gap between the poor and the poverty line is also smaller. But estimated poverty headcounts have risen. Using data from the same region and time period, we discuss whether this is significant progress from two perspectives.

Design decisions and rationale

  1. Lead with two measures that improved

    -1

    We show both the poverty rate and the depth of poverty, strengthening the case for progress in poverty reduction. This allows you to see both what proportion of people are below the poverty line and how far the poor are, on average, from that line. However, this graph does not show the increase in the number of people in poverty, so it is biased in favor of the proposition. We have also considered FOR 1 showing only the poverty rate. That graph is simpler, but lacks information about the depth of poverty.

  2. Compare the first and last years

    -0.5

    We compare 1990 and 2018 using paired bars. This allows us to show the changes in both metrics in a small space and is straightforward to read. But looking only at the start and end points misses changes in the middle, such as an early rise in the poverty rate and a later period when the decline slowed. FOR 1 retains the full annual variation. We ended up with this version because it makes it easier to compare poverty rates and poverty depths.

  3. Calculate shortfall among poor people

    +2

    The original poverty gap index also included non-poor people in the average, but recorded their gap as zero. Therefore, it is not equal to the average gap of the poor themselves. So, we divided the poverty gap index by the poverty rate and multiplied by 100 to estimate how far poor people are below the poverty line on average, as a percentage of that line. This can show the level of poverty. Using the raw index directly would have made the calculation easier, but it counted everyone and answered a different question. The percentages in the raw data are rounded, so the results are estimates. In addition, the people in poverty in different years are not necessarily the same people, so the results cannot be used to judge that the situation of the same people has actually improved.

  4. Use zero-based bars with clear denominators

    +1.5

    The same scale is used for both rows of bars, both starting from zero. This allows a direct comparison of bar lengths and does not exaggerate the decrease. If you cut off part of the axis, the change will appear larger, but the bar lengths will be misleading. We also made clear the different meanings of the two rows of percentages: the poverty rate is the percentage of the population living in poverty; The average gap is the ratio of the gap to the poverty line.

  5. Label the size of each improvement

    +1

    We directly plot the values and relative reductions so you don't have to do the math. Drops of 26.7% and 16.7% highlight improvements, so the starting and ending points are also included for easy checking. The caption states that the poverty rate fell by 14.7 percentage points to avoid mixing percentage points with relative declines. It would be more concise to label just the start and end points, but you would have to calculate the decrease yourself.

The case against the proposition

155 million more people lived in extreme poverty

Open full-size chart

Design decisions and rationale

  1. Estimate headcounts from matching records

    +2

    To estimate how many people were below the poverty line in a given year, we divided the poverty rate in the SSF area by 100 and multiplied it by the total population in that year. The poverty rate and total population data were available for each of the 29 years from 1990 to 2018, so there was no need to fill in missing data. This converts the ratio to the number of people. But the poverty rate is rounded, and the total population is an estimate, so the figure explicitly says "estimated people." Looking at the poverty rate eliminates this step, but it doesn't tell you how many people are still below the poverty line.

  2. Judge progress by the number still poor

    -1

    The opposing argument is more supported by the increase in the number of poor people rather than the poverty rate. This design makes readers notice first how many people still need help, rather than the proportion of these people in the total population. What changes is how progress is measured, not whether improvements in poverty rate and poverty depth are real.Focusing solely on the poverty rate highlights the proportion of the population affected by poverty. We chose the absolute number of poor people to make the opposing side pay more attention to the actual scale of the population affected by the poverty issue.

  3. Keep all 29 years and the zero baseline

    +1.5

    We present all 29 years of data, allowing readers to observe how the number of poor people has changed and reached the 2018 level, rather than merely comparing two years. The shaded filled area starts from the zero baseline , so the height of each year's graph is proportional to the number of poor people in that year. Another candidate design AGAINST 2 uses two stacked bar charts to show population growth, supplementing the background information on the overall population change. However, this design can only compare the starting year and the ending year, and cannot show the year-to-year change process .

  4. Emphasize the added human burden

    -0.5

    We use shaded area below the line and adopt the title "155 million more people lived in extreme poverty" , making the growth more obvious. The title rounds the estimated increase in population from 154.9 million to 155 million, while retaining the data of the starting and ending years and the 55.2% growth rate. This emphasis method may cause readers to overlook the fact that the poverty rate has decreased, so we explain in the chart caption that these two changes can occur simultaneously. We also considered an unfilled line chart without shading. An unfilled line chart would be simpler, but the shaded area better highlights the scale of poverty.

  5. Keep the historical line and limits visible

    +2

    We indicate in the source notes of the data source the used historical version, as well as the daily $1.90 poverty line calculated based on 2011 purchasing power parity . We use the same measurement standard for all years to avoid mixing different versions of the poverty line. These results can only reflect the situation of this region during this historical period and cannot represent the current poverty situation or explain which policy led to these changes . If a newer version of the poverty line is used, the comparison results will also change. Therefore, both FOR and AGAINST charts continue to use the definitions in the course data to ensure consistency.

Final reflection

Two true trends, two standards of progress

The calculation itself isn't complicated once you've matched records by region and year. It is harder to decide what constitutes "significant progress". The poverty rate has fallen and the average shortfall among poor people has narrowed, but the estimated number of people in poverty has increased. To our surprise, these results can hold simultaneously. Because the total population has more than doubled, even as the poverty rate has fallen, the number of poor people is still higher. So, one metric getting better doesn't answer the whole question directly.

Comparing the four candidates gives us a better idea of what each technique is keeping and what it is missing. FOR 1 is the easiest to read and also retains the full change in the poverty rate. FOR 2 requires a little more explanation, but it adds information on the depth of poverty. AGAINST 2 directly showing the total population growth also shows a large increase in the number of people at or above the poverty line. AGAINST 1 retains the estimated number of people in poverty in all 29 years, which is more suitable for expressing the contrary point of view. Finally, we choose FOR 2 and AGAINST 1. This allows both sides to make their case without changing region, period or poverty line.

After making these comparisons, we felt that it was not enough to check that the calculations were correct. The choice of which metric to display can already affect how the reader interprets "progress." Just because there are more people in poverty doesn't mean no one benefits; Nor does a falling poverty rate mean fewer people need help. The same goes for the depth of poverty, which compares the poor population in each year, not the same people. We believe that it is possible to explain why you value a certain metric more than others, but also explain the unit, formula, poverty line, and background that is not shown. It's misleading to think of one metric as the entire answer to a question.

Sources and calculations

One region, one period, one historical line

We use the World Bank Human Development Indicators historical data provided by the course. The region is sub-Saharan Africa, the code is SSF, and the years are 1990 to 2018. The indicator code for the poverty rate is SI.POV.DDAY, the poverty gap index is SI.POV.GAPS, and the total population is SP.POP.TOTL. The poverty indicator is from the 2021 version of the data, using a daily poverty line of $1.90 at 2011 purchasing power parity.

The poverty gap index averages the relative gap of all residents, and the gap of non-poor residents is recorded as zero. We use two formulas:

Estimated number of poor = poverty rate ÷100× total population in the same area in the same year.

Average gap in poverty (percentage of poverty line) = poverty gap index ÷ poverty rate ×100.

The percentages in the historical data are rounded, so the results are estimates. No uncertainty intervals are provided for the data. Regional aggregation can also hide differences between countries and cannot be used to determine causality.
